## Novelty (6): 
I found no *published* per-cart data on cart pose versus service time for automated side-loaders. The question is concrete, and its answer can come out either way, which the brief rewards. But the data is not "data nobody has". It is data the haulers already collect and keep private. Truck camera and telematics systems such as Heil's 3rd Eye Verif-Eye advertise timestamped, geolocated records of every lift. The snippet says they report "the time spent by the vehicle at each service stop" and flag misplaced carts ([3rd Eye refuse fleet systems](https://www.3rdeyecam.com/refuse-fleet-management-systems/)). Routeware and Rubicon integrate the same records. Eagle Vision's CartSeeker was co-developed with the City of Guelph, and the snippet describes it as estimating each cart's X, Y, Z and running the arm autonomously ([Waste & Recycling Mag](https://wasterecyclingmag.ca/tag/cartseeker)). There is also a Guelph thesis, "Automated Curbside Waste Collection using Advanced Computer Vision Strategies", reporting ~98% cart detection ([Atrium](https://atrium.lib.uoguelph.ca/items/17093316-8755-453f-845b-c7b59ee4402f), snippet only). Patents cover "automated alignment and dumping of refuse cans" (US11899460) and "detecting objects near the cart" (US12450914), titles only. So OEMs very likely know their arm's pose tolerance. The new part is a public tolerance curve under human operators, which is a real but incremental, operations-flavoured contribution. To an IROS/CASE robotics reviewer it reads as a time-and-motion study, not a new robotics finding.

## Feasibility (5.5): 
The good parts: the hardware is cheap and simple, nobody enters the roadway, and a first-light plot by Dec 20 is plausible if households sign up in October. The measurement and inference plan has several serious problems, though:

1. **The sensor cannot hit the stated pose accuracy.** The LD19's own spec is ±45 mm ranging accuracy, 0.8° angular resolution at 10 Hz, ±2° angle error, and ambient-light tolerance of only 30 kLux ([Waveshare wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/DTOF_LIDAR_LD19)). The proposal's target of <3 cm / <3° cannot be met. At 10–15 m along a 25–35 m frontage, a 60 cm cart gets about 3–5 points. A 40 cm-high scan along a row of carts sees mostly one grazing face and suffers heavy cart-to-cart occlusion, so yaw and spacing (the key covariates) will be the noisiest. The 5 am–2 pm window includes direct midday sun, which is well above 30 kLux.
2. **Truck, driver, site and stream are nearly perfectly confounded.** Each site-stream is one route with the same driver every week, so the study has about 7–9 clusters. Natural pose is also confounded with household habit and with parked cars and traffic. The truck's stop choice depends on the same things.
3. **The power calculation ignores clustering.** With ~15 carts per driver-day and a plausible ICC of 0.2, the design effect is about 3.8, so 1,300 events act like roughly 340. Natural poses also cluster near "compliant", so the GAM has little support in the tails, which is exactly where the threshold lives.
4. **The event count is optimistic.** 6–10 houses with real set-out rates give perhaps 5–8 carts per stream, not 15. Twelve collection days per site requires all stations live by late November and running through holiday schedule shifts and the rainy season.
5. **Ethics are not trivially exempt.** The designed arm deliberately changes a worker's task without the hauler's consent. The outcomes (per-stop dwell and re-grabs, keyed to route and date) are effectively a performance record for an identifiable driver. That puts the study outside the clean reading of exemption 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2)(ii), which requires that disclosure would not risk subjects' "employability" ([UMich exemption 2 guidance](https://hrpp.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2024/10/exemption_2_-_tips_and_examples.pdf)). A university IRB will not issue a determination for high-school investigators unless the lab is engaged in the research, which is not a "cheap ask".
6. **Safety and property risk.** Prescribed poses (0.2 m spacing, 60° yaw) invite knocked or tipped carts that end up in the street. Students must not retrieve them, and the protocol says nothing about this. Stations also need a stated standoff beyond arm reach and off the sidewalk.

## Impact (5.5): 
A tolerance spec for bin-robot placement is useful to this team and a few cart-robot startups. But three routes and 9 driver-truck pairs, all from one region's hauler(s), cannot support a "cities' rules are over- or under-specified" claim that a hauler or city would act on. Haulers with telematics could refute or replace it with one query. The OEM audience (CartSeeker) is already automating the very human operator the curve describes. Realistically this is a CASE workshop or WIP paper, or a *Waste Management* short note. It is not CASE main track unless a hauler provides the data.

## Lab appeal: 
Carpin's stochastic orienteering line (CASE 2025, per labs.md) could in principle use stochastic service-time distributions. But per-cart arm handling time has no obvious place in an orienteering benchmark, and 9 clusters is too thin to be a benchmark. Wu's link (parking-vehicle docking tolerance, roadside cooperative perception) is loose: a 2D lidar on a tripod in a yard is not the intersection-scale perception her NSF project studies. Both labs would see little gain for the IRB exposure the study carries.

## Main criticism: 
The design tries to estimate a causal pose→service-time effect from passive observation of about 8 confounded driver-route clusters, using a sensor whose accuracy is coarser than the effects of interest. Meanwhile, the people who own the right data (haulers, with per-lift camera and telematics records, plus their consent) are not part of the plan. The result will probably be descriptive and underpowered, with a non-trivial human-subjects problem.

## What would raise the score: 
1. **Partner with the local hauler or city in October** (e.g., the franchise hauler for the team's city). Ask for anonymized per-lift timestamps or Verif-Eye-style stop records across many routes, and use the yard stations only to measure the pose ground truth those records lack. This fixes the cluster count, the consent problem and the impact problem in one step.
2. **Make the designed arm the primary experiment, run with hauler approval.** Use a pre-registered, small grid on spacing only (the one factor the proposal itself says may dominate), with drivers informed. Size it with a mixed-model power analysis that treats driver-day as the cluster.
3. **Fix the sensing.** Mount the station high and look down on the curb line (a 2D lidar tilted, or a camera with fiducial ground truth). Restrict each station to about 10 m of frontage. Validate yaw and spacing error across the actual field of view, not at one calibration spot, and report it against the expected effect size.
4. **Get a written IRB or SRC determination before any data collection.** Collect no per-driver or per-route identifiers, and agree with the hauler on how dwell data may be used.
5. **Write a safety protocol:** station standoff distance, no retrieval from the street, a cap on the designed poses most likely to tip a cart into the roadway, and a host-household contact for every collection day.
6. **Narrow the claim:** "placement-accuracy spec for cart-moving robots under human-operated ASLs, n routes." Drop "cities' rules are unjustified" unless the data spans several haulers.

## Closest prior work found: 
- [3rd Eye (Heil) refuse fleet systems / Verif-Eye](https://www.3rdeyecam.com/refuse-fleet-management-systems/) — per-lift timestamped records, time per service stop, and misplaced-cart flags (snippet). Haulers already hold the data this study would collect.
- [Routeware](https://routeware.com/?p=12176) — truck cameras capture each service event, tied to stop, time and location (snippet).
- [CartSeeker / Eagle Vision, Guelph](https://wasterecyclingmag.ca/tag/cartseeker) — vision estimates cart X, Y, Z and the arm grabs autonomously, so OEMs work with pose tolerance internally.
- ["Automated Curbside Waste Collection using Advanced Computer Vision Strategies," U. Guelph](https://atrium.lib.uoguelph.ca/items/17093316-8755-453f-845b-c7b59ee4402f) — cart detection for automated arms, ~98% detection (snippet only; page blocked).
- US11899460 "Automated alignment and dumping of refuse cans"; US12450914 "Detecting objects near the cart" — patents on alignment and obstacles near the cart (titles only; pages blocked).
- [FleetRabbit ASL guide](https://fleetrabbit.com/blogs/post/automated-side-loader-asl-garbage-truck-guide) — 8–12 s cycle, 250–350 realistic lifts per hour (low authority; same source the proposal uses).
- Everett et al. 1996 (J. Environ. Eng.) — placement-agnostic route-time models, cited correctly in the proposal as the baseline.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal: 
- The "target error under 3 cm, under 3°" for pose from an LD19 conflicts with the sensor's own spec: ±45 mm accuracy, 0.8° resolution, ±2° angle error ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/DTOF_LIDAR_LD19)).
- "Lidar is light-independent": the LD19 is rated for only 30 kLux ambient light, which is below direct midday sun, and the stations run until 2 pm.
- "Nobody has published which rule terms actually cost time" is literally true, but it is misleading as stated. Hauler and OEM telematics already record per-stop time and misplaced carts, so the data exists privately. The proposal's "unsure" item 6 understates this.
- "~15 carts per stream per site" is likely optimistic for 6–10 houses once set-out rates are counted, so the ≈1,300-event total is overstated.
- "Ask a host lab's IRB office for an exemption determination… a cheap ask" understates the issue. Driver performance data falls under the employability clause of exemption (d)(2)(ii), and an IRB generally reviews only its own institution's investigators.
