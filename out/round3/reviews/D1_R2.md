# D1 — Reviewer 2 (Methodology rigor and feasibility for this team)
Scores: N 6 / F 5.5 / I 5.5 → overall 5.67

Search note: I used 14 web searches. As in the proposal, full paper pages could not be opened from this sandbox, so every claim below comes from search-result snippets.

## Novelty (6): 
I found no published 2×2 that crosses leaf-surface water (dew or mist) with soil water for a small driven wheel on turf. The searches turned up only maker blog advice ("wet grass reduces traction", e.g. [Navimow](https://navimow.com/pages/robotic-lawn-mowers-for-slopes-and-hills)) and patents on slip detection, so the specific question looks open. However, nearby facts are well known. Water films lower friction on grass for smooth soles, and protrusions or lugs restore it ([JEST artificial-grass friction paper](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_79983.html)). Soil-water effects on turf traction have been studied and conflict in sign ([PSU SSRC](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf/@@download/file/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf)). Species effects on tyre traction on turf are documented ([Cenek et al.](https://trid.trb.org/View/783691); a Teka single-wheel turf study in the results). Single-wheel testers on turf are standard agricultural equipment. So the "tread × film" interaction outcome is close to predictable from the sole-friction literature: smooth tyres lose grip under a film, lugs do not. A main-track reviewer would likely read this as "turf terramechanics in a new setting, plus a factorial" rather than a new question. The claim that vision slip models are "structurally blind" to wetness would be the novel part, but only if the within-lawn share turns out large and is measured in a way that does not depend on the design (see below).

## Feasibility (5.5): 
The hardware is mostly fine. By my own rough estimate (not verified), hoverboard hub motors at about 15 A give several N·m per wheel, which is enough to spin a 15 kg robot on dry turf. So the stated "main risk" is probably not the real one. The real risks are in the method and the logistics:
- **No slip curve from a fixed stake.** Tethering the robot to a fixed stake through a load cell gives a stalled-wheel (100% slip) peak force. It does not give a pull-versus-slip curve or "slip at 90% of peak", because the robot cannot move. Real slip curves need a moving, controllable load, such as a towed braked cart. That is the proposed fallback, and it should be the primary design.
- **Too much field work for two part-timers.** The plan is 1,340 pulls and 56 lawn-visits at 5–6 visits a week across December finals and winter break. Every slip pull tears up turf on 14 lawns that belong to other people. This is the same consent problem the generator used to kill K8.
- **Dew visits in the dark.** Dew visits at 6:45–7:30 am fall before sunrise in December and January in Northern California.
- **Rainy-season soil states.** In the rainy season the "as-found" soil will often already be wet. The soil contrast will shrink, or the "dry soil" cell will be impossible to get for weeks. Misting in the afternoon is a cleaner manipulation, but mist is not dew, which also comes with cold leaves and turgor.
- **Held-out test too small.** Only 4 held-out lawns: a cluster bootstrap over 4 clusters cannot support an RMSE ranking of four models.
- **Power analysis covers the wrong test.** It powers only the easy main effect of leaf wetness. The interactions and the variance-share headline are not powered.

## Impact (5.5): 
A measured dew/soil/tread split with a slope-derate number would be useful to robot-mower firmware teams and would be a reusable dataset. But the impact the proposal claims depends on two headline outputs that, as designed, are weak:
- **The variance share is set by the design.** The "within-lawn temporal share" is fixed by the factor levels the team chooses (how much they mist, how much they irrigate). It is not the natural share across real hours and days. A reader cannot carry it over to deployment.
- **The free-weather model cannot see the manipulations.** The M2 model gets CIMIS dew point and hours since rain, but those inputs know nothing about the team's misting or irrigation. So M2 can only be scored fairly on natural-state cells, which are a minority of the data.

Without fixes, this is a solid CASE-workshop or ICRA-workshop dataset paper, not an IROS traversability result.

## Lab appeal: 
Carpin's lab is a reasonable fit. The ICRA 2024 Nav2 local-costmap paper (Sani, Sgorbissa, Carpin, [arXiv 2407.18535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18535)) is about letting robots drive through tall grass that lidar sees as an obstacle. A traction-versus-moisture link is a natural next layer on that, though the lab's interest is vineyards, not lawns. I could not confirm the Kobayashi SIP 2026 project ECE-08 from search snippets. If it exists as described, a ready-made traction rig and dataset would cost that lab little. Neither lab gets much from the residential-lawn framing itself.

## Main criticism: 
The design does not let the headline claims be answered as stated. A fixed-stake tether cannot produce slip curves. The variance decomposition measures the team's chosen manipulation ranges rather than natural temporal variation. The "free weather data predicts held-out lawns" test is blind to the misting and irrigation that create most of the wetness variation, and it is scored on only 4 lawns. All of this runs on a 56-visit, 1,340-pull field campaign that damages neighbours' turf.

## What would raise the score: 
1. Make a towed single-wheel tester with a controllable brake (or a robot towing a braked trailer) the primary instrument. Log true slip from a ground-speed encoder, and drop the fixed stake.
2. Split the study into two parts:
   - (a) A small controlled factorial (mist × irrigation × tread) on 3–4 own or school lawns, to establish the mechanism.
   - (b) A natural-state observational panel: same spots, repeated at pre-set times (dawn, mid-morning, afternoon) on random days, with no manipulation. The temporal variance share and the M2 free-weather model must come only from (b).
3. Cut to about 8 lawns that the team or the school controls, or where owners accept turf damage. Use 1–2 pulls per cell, and run the power analysis on the variance-share CI and on the held-out RMSE difference, not on the main effect.
4. Use leave-one-lawn-out CV over all lawns rather than 4 fixed held-out lawns, and report a lawn-level cluster bootstrap.
5. Replace morning dew visits before dawn with weekend dawn sessions, plus afternoon mist sessions, so they fit a school schedule.
6. Drop or reframe M4 (μ from 3 m of normal driving). Peak friction is not observable without slip excitation (a standard result in tyre-road friction estimation), so M4 needs a deliberate excitation manoeuvre.
7. Fix the slope check to μ·cosθ ≥ sinθ + c_rr·cosθ. Make sure the slopes chosen actually reach the limit (at μ ≈ 0.6 the limit is around 50% grade, above most natural 10–30% lawn slopes), or add towed load to simulate steeper grades.

## Closest prior work found:
- [PSU SSRC turf traction (PennFoot)](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf/@@download/file/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf): soil water versus traction, with species-dependent sign. Covers the soil side for cleats, not wheels or leaf film.
- [Cenek, Jamieson & McLarin, roadside grass friction](https://trid.trb.org/View/783691): dry versus wet, species, and a lightly loaded tyre that is more wetness-sensitive. Closest on the wetness and species side.
- [JEST, friction sliding against (wet) artificial grass](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_79983.html): a water film cuts friction for flat soles, and protrusions restore it. This anticipates the tread × film interaction.
- [Eder et al., TU Graz, RAS 168 (2023)](https://graz.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/traversability-analysis-for-off-road-environments-using-locomotio): standardized locomotion experiments and EO data for per-terrain costs across 4 robots. No explicit wetness factor found in the snippets.
- [SlipNet, arXiv 2409.02273](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02273v2) and Angelova et al. RSS 2006: image and terrain-class slip prediction. These are the baselines the idea challenges.
- Teka single-wheel test of tyres on turf of differing species composition (in the [Teka 13(1) pdf](https://agro.icm.edu.pl/agro/element/bwmeta1.element.agro-bf395dba-74bf-4e6d-a950-1e51b8ecfa10/c/9_Teka_13_1.pdf)): an established single-wheel-on-turf method. I could not open it to check details.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal:
- **Wrong authors.** "Fritz et al. (TU Graz), RAS 2023" names the wrong people. The snippets give the authors as Eder, Prinz, Schöggl and Steinbauer-Wagner (RAS vol. 168, Oct 2023).
- **Measurement claim does not hold.** A fixed ground-stake tether is said to yield "the pull-versus-slip curve ... and slip at 90% of peak". It cannot, because the robot does not move.
- **Slope formula.** "sin θ + c_rr ≈ μ_peak·cos θ" leaves out cosθ on the rolling-resistance term.
- **Main risk misidentified (my own estimate).** The stated main risk (a 15 A current limit reached before peak traction at 15 kg) looks unlikely for hoverboard hub motors.
- **Not confirmed:** the Kobayashi SIP 2026 ECE-08 project and the "about $150 research-grade leaf-wetness sensor" price did not show up in my searches. The Carpin ICRA 2024 paper and the Cenek "lightly loaded tyre more wetness-sensitive" finding are correctly described.
