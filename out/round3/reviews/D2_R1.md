## Novelty (6): Real gap, but it is an operations time study rather than new robotics, and it is closer to known work than the proposal says.
- **The gap is real.** After about 20 searches I found no public curve of service time against cart pose (distance, yaw, spacing) for automated side-loaders (ASLs). The team's own prior-art sweep (out/prior_art.md, lines 218 and 1485) found the same thing.
- **The question is less untouched than the proposal implies:**
  - **Duke/Fayetteville (2016).** Duke Sanford students and Fayetteville NC Environmental Services ran a randomized field experiment on ASL cart placement: letter vs. cart sticker vs. both, explaining that handles facing the curb, away from mailboxes and trees, "made collection faster" (Waste Dive and Route Fifty snippets). It measured compliance, apparently not seconds. It is the closest field study of placement in ASL collection, and the proposal misses it.
  - **Field time studies.** These already report about 11–12 s per residence for automated residual collection and 13–17 s for recyclables, with lifting/idle time per stop of about 8.3–8.7 s. The snippet seems to come from a Resources, Conservation & Recycling 2016 (vol. 114, pp. 92–102) fuel-and-time study; I could not open it.
  - **Telematics patents.** Several patents on segmenting stop time from waste-truck GPS give "16 s per collection, 26 s for exceptions".
  - **Private data.** ASL telematics already timestamp every arm cycle from arm micro-switches or pressure transducers (fleet-software snippets). Large haulers and OEMs (CartSeeker logs cart angle and proximity) almost certainly hold private data that answers this question. So the novelty is "first public data", not "first knowledge".
- **On paper the effect is half-predictable.** "Out of arm reach → truck repositions" and "touching carts → two cycles" follow from arm geometry. The truly uncertain parts are where the yaw and spacing thresholds sit, and how much drivers compensate.
- **Venue fit.** A CASE reviewer will see a careful industrial-engineering time study, which is new in this venue but not a new robotics method or finding. The "truck stopping decision dominates" hypothesis is the most interesting angle and should lead the paper.

## Feasibility (5)
- **Hardware is low-risk.** The real risks are statistical and logistical.
- **The headline metric is the wrong one.** "Handling time = cart footprint vanishes to reappears" is mostly the fixed hydraulic lift-dump-return cycle. Placement mainly changes the *pre-grab* phase: truck positioning, arm extension, re-grabs, and the driver leaving the cab. A yard-side 2D lidar at 40 cm will see the cart, but the truck body and arm sit behind it from the station's view. Truck dwell per cart and approach time need to be the primary outcomes, and the proposal has not checked that a single 2D scan plane can segment them reliably.
- **Pseudo-replication.** Each site sees the same 1–3 drivers every week. The "n ≈ 1,300" is really about 9 clusters, and the power calculation ignores the intra-class correlation (ICC). With driver effects of a few seconds, the effective sample for a pose effect is far smaller than claimed, and a GAM with three smooths plus random intercepts on 9 levels will be fragile.
- **Observational data has thin tails.** Residents mostly place carts roughly as the rules say, so the threshold region relies on the small designed arm (~150 events, about 50 per level). Drivers who see the same odd 60°/0.2 m placements every week may adapt, get out to fix carts, or report the household to the hauler.
- **Data volume is optimistic.** Recycling and green waste are often collected every other week, and holidays shift schedules. ~12 collection days per site from Dec to Feb is optimistic.
- **Human subjects and consent.** Timing identifiable route drivers is borderline for "trivially exempt", and recruiting 3–4 host households plus consenting neighbours by Oct 31 is a real dependency.
- **No hauler partner.** Without one, the team cannot know grabber type, driver mode (manual vs. assisted), or whether skipped carts were deliberate.

## Impact (5.5)
- **Audience.** If the curve is clean, cities and haulers would cite it, and it supplies the placement spec for every bin-to-curb robot.
- **Generalization ceiling.** About 9 truck-driver pairs, probably one hauler, and one region. A tolerance curve tied to one arm model and one driver culture is hard to reuse.
- **Shelf life.** The curve applies to human-operated arms just as OEMs move to automatic grab (CartSeeker, Heil, Waterloo Controls). An automatic arm's tolerance region will differ, so the result's shelf life is short unless at least one site uses an assisted arm.
- **Venue.** CASE main track is a stretch for a pure observational time study, and the natural readers (Waste Management, J. Environ. Eng.) are not the target venue.
- **Rule classifier.** The "city rule as a binary classifier" baseline is a nice touch, but with 9 clusters the held-out validation on a 4th station is one more cluster, not real held-out generalization.

## Lab appeal
- **Carpin.** The link is thin: a stochastic-orienteering group needs service-time distributions, but would not get much from 9 trucks' per-cart times when synthetic distributions serve its benchmarks.
- **Wu.** Calling a curbside lidar logger "cooperative roadside perception", or a docking analogue, is a stretch.
- **Better fit.** A transportation or operations group, or a hauler such as GreenWaste/Recology in the Bay Area, would value this more. Neither UC Merced nor SJSU is likely to invest much beyond light advice.

## Main criticism
- **Question vs. design.** The question is valid, but the proposed measurement cannot answer it well. The metric (cart vanish→reappear) misses the placement-sensitive phase, and the data are about 9 driver clusters dressed up as n ≈ 1,300.
- **Private data.** Haulers and OEMs likely already hold far better data (arm-cycle telematics, CartSeeker logs). Without a hauler partner, the public result will be small, regional, and easy to dismiss.

## What would raise the score
1. **Get a hauler or city partner** (e.g., GreenWaste, Recology, or a municipal fleet) for arm-cycle telematics or in-cab video of a few routes. This multiplies the clusters, removes the roadside-sensing burden, and makes the team's lidar stations a validation layer.
2. **Redefine outcomes.** Primary outcomes should be truck dwell per cart, arm-approach time (first arm motion to grab), and re-grab or driver-exit counts. Show on the home curb in November that a 2D scan (or a second, higher lidar) can segment these phases.
3. **Redo the power analysis with driver clustering.** Report effective sample size under plausible ICCs (0.1–0.3), and give the number of distinct drivers needed. Aim for ≥20 driver-truck pairs across ≥2 haulers and both grabber styles.
4. **Lead with the falsifiable mechanism.** "Spacing/pairing via the stop decision dominates distance-to-curb" should be stated as a pre-registered contrast, not a byproduct of a GAM.
5. **Include at least one automated or assisted-grab truck,** so the curve informs the automation transition rather than describing a vanishing regime.
6. **Cite and build on prior work:** the Duke/Fayetteville placement experiment, the existing per-residence ASL time studies, and the GPS stop-time literature. Position the work as conditioning their stop-time distributions on pose.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal
- **"Arm reach is 8–12 ft."** Not supported by what I found. One ASL model is quoted at 88 in (about 7.3 ft) of reach, with an 8–20 s cycle, so 12 ft looks high for typical arms. Cite a spec sheet.
- **"Nobody has published which rule terms actually cost time" / "Rules are stated without evidence."** Overstated. The Duke/Fayetteville field experiment exists, even though it measured compliance rather than seconds. Per-residence ASL timing studies also exist, without pose covariates.
- **"8–12 s per dump cycle."** This comes from a low-authority fleet-software blog. The Recycling Product News spec (8–20 s) and the per-residence timing studies are better sources.
- **"Carpin CASE 2018 Best Paper".** Unverified (CV not reachable here). The proposal should check it before using it.
- **Links I could not open.** CartSeeker's "96% recognition, 8% cost reduction", the patent numbers US10358287 (Con-Tech, per out/prior_art.md), US10974895 and US11332307, and the Amarillo, Cranbrook and Sammamish links were all egress-blocked. None were found wrong, but I could not verify them.
