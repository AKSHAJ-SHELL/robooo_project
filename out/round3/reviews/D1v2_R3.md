# D1.v2 — Reviewer 3 (Impact, venue fit and lab appeal)
Scores: N 6 / F 6.5 / I 5.5 → overall 6.00

## Novelty (6): 
The v2 framing is honest and better. It is a falsifiable test of whether the UTK lawn-tractor finding transfers to robot-scale wheel loads, with leaf water and soil water separated. My search confirms the UTK precedent (search snippet of SAE 911240, Tompkins, Freeland, Wilhelm & Sarten 1991): "average drive wheel slip was reduced when water was sprayed on the grass surface" and "wet turf produced less rear wheel slip than dry". So the sign question is real and has an anchor. I found no peer-reviewed robot-mower or small-UGV study that measures μ–slip on living turf with leaf and soil water separated. The robot-mower web content is marketing (for example, a Lymow blog's unsourced "moisture reduces tire grip by 40–60%"), which shows the gap exists. Still, to a robotics reviewer this is "known terramechanics/turf-traction method, new load regime plus one extra factor", i.e. 5–6 on the rubric. The "hidden temporal state for image-based traversability" angle is the most novel part to robotics readers. But that idea is already widely stated qualitatively (WayFAST-style work notes that weather changes traversability without changing appearance), and with 8 lawns the paper can only give a wide-CI share, not a strong finding.

## Feasibility (6.5): 
The towed carriage with a torque-mode hub wheel, fifth wheel and hitch load cell is the right instrument. The torque needed is small (about 36 N × 0.08 m ≈ 3 N·m), so it is within hoverboard FOC capability. The load is heavy for two part-time students: about 144 factorial ramps, 480 panel ramps, 36 mist-versus-dew ramps, 72 slope stalls, about 25 one-hour panel sessions including dawns, plus gravimetric calibration and a new rig built from scratch by Dec 6. Two seasonal factors cut against the design. (1) Dec–Feb is the Northern California rainy season, so "as-found" soil will often already be wet. That is good for the panel but erodes the Part A soil contrast. (2) Warm-season lawns (kikuyu, Bermuda), which the proposal wants as the species contrast, are dormant or semi-dormant in winter, so the species factor will be confounded with dormancy. Ramp noise on uneven home lawns is untested until late November, and the go/no-go and fallbacks are reasonable. The mist-versus-dew check is admittedly only able to detect gross mismatches.

## Impact (5.5): 
**Venue fit is the weak point.** CASE main track wants automation science or engineering. A turf-traction measurement study with a design rule is closer to *Journal of Terramechanics*, *Biosystems Engineering* or ASABE than to CASE/IROS. A clean sign reversal plus a tread rule could make a CASE short paper. IROS is unlikely, as the proposal itself concedes. **Maker uptake:** Husqvarna, Segway/Navimow and others already sell terrain kits and lugged wheels for "wet grass" and run internal slope tests. A leaf-versus-soil derate rule is actionable in principle, but a high-school study on 8 California lawns with one 6.5" hoverboard tyre is unlikely to change firmware. The more realistic outcome is a citation in a blog or white paper. **Consumer testers:** my search snippet shows Consumer NZ's damp-grass item is one of its *cutting-performance* assessments, listed separately from the 19°/35% slope cutting test. Their "damp" condition is therefore about cut quality and clumping, not traction. The proposed "protocol fix" partly misses their purpose. **Traversability researchers:** about 600 ramps from one carriage wheel (not a robot driving its own path), with a downward still and a short clip, is too small and too unlike WayFAST-style driving logs to be a dataset others adopt. Its value is as a sanity-check table. Overall this is a strong workshop paper or a modest CASE paper with a design rule. The audience is narrow but real.

## Lab appeal: 
Weak to moderate. Kitts's RSL does run an agricultural robot (search snippets: an "agbot" being tested at a San Jose farm, autonomously navigating crop rows) and the modular rover (IMECE 2023). But I found no traction or terramechanics line there, and recent publications I could find are swarm and multi-robot control. The realistic ask is a friendly one-hour visit and feedback, not co-authorship or equipment. For Elkaim, my searches surfaced only older GNC and Overbot off-road work and SIP Nav2 projects, no traction research. The proposal honestly calls this "a bridge to future work". Dropping Kobayashi seems wrong for this topic: his SIP 2026 project (ECE-08, per labs.md) is literally UGV terrain traversability with slip and slope. Even if SIP is after the deadline, he is the one reachable faculty member whose stated interest matches this idea.

## Main criticism: 
The impact story leans on audiences that are unlikely to act on it. Makers have their own test regimes. Consumer NZ's damp test is about cutting, not traction. The dataset is too small and too rig-specific for traversability researchers to reuse. The named labs have no active traction line, so they gain little and will probably offer advice only. What remains is a careful, publishable terramechanics-in-a-new-regime result whose natural home is a terramechanics or ag-engineering venue rather than CASE/IROS main track.

## What would raise the score:
1. Get one real user before submission. For example, ask one robot-mower maker's support or test team (or the borrowed-mower owner) whether a leaf-versus-soil derate rule would change their guidance, or run the borrowed commercial mower climb as a required (not optional) check so the result is shown on a product.
2. Re-target lab outreach to Kobayashi (UCSC, traversability with slip and slope) as the topical advisor. Keep Kitts for a hardware visit and frame the ask as "test your agbot's wheel on our carriage", which gives RSL data on its own platform.
3. Recast the consumer-tester hook correctly: propose adding a *traction/slope-in-damp* condition, since Consumer NZ currently tests damp-grass cutting and slope cutting separately. Or drop the hook.
4. Make the dataset match how traversability models are actually trained. Log the robot base driving the same strips (proprioceptive slip from the bin-robot's wheel speed versus fifth wheel) alongside carriage μ, so WayFAST-style traction estimates can be compared against physical μ at each wetness state.
5. Choose cool-season species only (or log dormancy explicitly), so the species factor is not confounded with winter dormancy. Trim the panel to protect Part A, which carries the headline sign result.
6. Name *Journal of Terramechanics* / ASABE as a co-primary target, with a CASE short paper framed around the "wet-turf derate rule plus test method".

## Closest prior work found:
- Tompkins et al., SAE 911240 (1991), https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/911240/ : lawn-tractor tyres on turf; water spray reduced slip (search snippet). This is the direct anchor; the proposal cites it correctly.
- UTK MS thesis, "Evaluation of turf tire sipe density on traction performance and turf damage", https://trace.tennessee.edu/entities/publication/16d2bdaa-c02d-4964-8f31-dd6fd3a3fe47 : Ford lawn tractor on tall fescue with a constant drawbar load (snippet). Same method lineage at tractor scale.
- Cenek et al., frictional characteristics of roadside grass, https://trid.trb.org/View/783691 : grass friction versus wetness and species; dragged tyres more sensitive to wetness (snippet). Supports the "light load is more wetness-sensitive" hypothesis.
- PSU SSRC turf traction versus cutting height and soil water (proposal's link) : soil-water effect with a sign that varies by species, measured with cleats rather than wheels.
- WayFAST, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12071 : image-to-traction coefficient learned on grass and snow. The target audience for the "hidden temporal state" claim.
- Robot-mower patent EP4461114 (data.epo.org) and Husqvarna's "Control signal sensing for a robotic work tool" (USPTO 12075722): the latter's snippet says slip detection fails when "friction between grass and drive wheels is intrinsically low, such as … wet grass". This shows industry recognizes the problem but has not quantified it.
