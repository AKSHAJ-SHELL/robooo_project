## Novelty (6)
The framing is fresh for robotics: treating wetness as a hidden *temporal* state, splitting leaf film from soil water, and asking whether image-only slip models are blind to it. But the proposal misses the closest prior work. SAE Technical Paper 911240 (1991), "Tractive Performance of Ride-On Lawn and Garden Tractor Tires During Vehicle Operation on Turf", instrumented small tractors on turf, measuring slip, draft force and pitch. According to the search snippets, "average drive wheel slip was actually reduced when water was sprayed on the grass surface". Related University of Tennessee master's theses report that "operation on a wet turf surface produced less rear wheel slip than operation on a dry turf surface". That is a surface-water-versus-dry test for small driven wheels on turf, run decades ago, and it partly contradicts the proposal's hint that leaf film dominates. So "nobody appears to have run this" holds only for the full 2×2 with soil water separated. Other wetting protocols also exist. Sports-turf traction work routinely irrigates the surface just before testing to create a "wet" condition (PSU Supercleat report). Rail adhesion work has quantified wet versus dry leaf layers (wet CoT about 0.04 versus 0.06–0.11 dry; Jaffé et al. 2026). Using weather or precipitation data to set terrain moisture and slip risk is claimed in a family of US patents titled "Predicting terrain traversability for a vehicle" (US11193781, US11156469, US11698264). Robot-mower patents already use humidity or moisture thresholds, and one maker markets detection of "rising humidity and changes in ground traction". What is left is a careful multi-site small-robot dataset with the leaf/soil factorial and a held-out predictor comparison. That is a known effect measured in a new setting with a nicer decomposition: rubric 5–6, edging toward 6 for the temporal-state argument against vision slip models.

## Feasibility (6.5)
The rig is simple: a hoverboard, a load cell and a tethered drawbar ramp. The fallback (a towed braked single wheel) is standard. The hard parts are logistics and design validity:
- **Logistics.** The plan needs 14 recruited lawns, 56 lawn-visits (5–6 per week) and pre-dawn sessions for two part-time students over the holidays. Soil-wet visits need neighbours to irrigate on schedule. California winter rain will impose soil states the team does not control.
- **Mist versus dew.** Hand misting at 0.2–0.3 mm is a plausible stand-in for dew, but real dew sits on both leaf surfaces, comes with guttation and is spread through the canopy. Whether mist reproduces dew traction is itself unvalidated, so they need natural-dew versus mist control pulls on the same lawn.
- **Power analysis.** It is back-of-envelope. It is fine for the leaf contrast, but the held-out test has only 4 lawns. A ranking of M1–M4 on 4 lawns, where each lawn's species and soil are confounded, will have very wide CIs.
- **Drive limit.** Reaching peak traction on dry dense turf at 15 kg within the hoverboard current limit is a real risk. The team correctly scheduled it as an October de-risk.

## Impact (6.5)
If the within-lawn temporal swing turns out larger than the between-lawn swing, vision-only slip papers would have to cite and absorb that caveat, and mower makers would get a quantified dew-derate rule. But makers already advise waiting for dew to burn off (Navimow says until 10 am, and 4–6 h after sprinkling), already sell spiked wheels, and already gate on humidity or rain. A number for the derate is useful engineering, not a new capability. The headline "variance share" depends on the manipulation levels the experimenters choose (see Main criticism), which weakens its value as a design rule for the field. The released dataset (about 1,300 pulls with covariates) has modest reuse value. My best guess is a solid CASE paper or a good workshop paper. IROS main track is unlikely unless the image-only model is shown to fail badly on held-out lawns.

## Main criticism
The headline number, the "within-lawn temporal share versus between-lawn share" of traction variance, is largely produced by the factorial design. The team chooses how wet "wet" is (mist dose, irrigation volume) and samples leaf-wet and soil-wet states 50/50 by construction. The resulting variance share says nothing about how much traction actually varies over the hours and days a mower operates. It is a property of the manipulation levels, not of lawns. Combined with the missed lawn-tractor turf literature (SAE 911240, which found surface water *reduced* slip), the core claim is both less new and less meaningful than presented.

## What would raise the score
1. **Cite and position against the lawn-tractor turf work.** Use SAE 911240 and the UTK theses on lawn/garden tractor tires on turf, and the sports-turf wet-surface protocols. Reframe the question as: "the sign of the surface-water effect differs between a 200+ kg lawn tractor and a 10–15 kg robot, and with tread". That reframing turns the prior result into the motivation.
2. **Estimate the temporal share from natural states.** Separate the causal factorial (mechanism) from the variance decomposition (how much traction varies in practice). Base the decomposition on unmanipulated, as-found pulls at fixed times (for example 7 am, 11 am and 3 pm on several days per lawn). Report the natural share, and use the factorial only to explain it.
3. **Validate mist against natural dew.** Run natural-dew versus mist pulls with matched leaf-wetness readings on 2–3 lawns before relying on mist.
4. **Fix the held-out test.** Increase the held-out set or use leave-one-lawn-out across all 14 lawns, with species-stratified folds. Pre-register the M1–M4 ranking metric and the minimum RMSE gap that counts as "image-only is blind".
5. **Test against the field's real baseline.** Use the authors' own setting where possible, for example a SlipNet- or Angelova-style regressor with released code. Add a real commercial mower (borrowed Navimow or Husqvarna, stock versus spiked wheels) on the same strips, so the result speaks directly to the industry's slope ratings.

## Closest prior work found
- SAE 911240 (1991), "Tractive Performance of Ride-On Lawn and Garden Tractor Tires During Vehicle Operation on Turf" (https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/911240/). Small-vehicle tire slip and draft on turf. Water sprayed on the grass *reduced* slip (snippet). This is the closest and is missing from the proposal.
- University of Tennessee master's theses on lawn/garden tractor tire traction on turf (https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/7107, https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/6831). Snippet: "wet turf surface produced less rear wheel slip than... dry turf". These may be the same project as the SAE paper.
- PSU SSRC, McNitt et al., PennFoot traction versus cutting height and soil water (https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf/@@download/file/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf). Cited correctly; the species and cut-height effects are already known.
- PSU Supercleat report (https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/supercleat-report.pdf). A standard "wet" condition made by irrigating 10 minutes before testing, on Bermuda, bluegrass and ryegrass. This is surface-water manipulation on turf.
- Cenek et al., roadside grass friction (https://saferroadsconference.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Peter-Cenek-Frictional-Characteristics-Roadside-Grass-Types.pdf). Dry and wet friction by species. Cited; species effect confirmed in the snippet.
- Jaffé et al. 2026, leaf-layer adhesion, moisture the dominant factor (https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/240628/). Quantifies a wet versus dry leaf film on a contact, though wheel–rail rather than turf.
- US patents "Predicting terrain traversability for a vehicle" (US11193781, US11156469, US11698264; https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11193781). Moisture from weather/precipitation data plus geo-referenced slip labels for a slip prediction model. Close to the M2 idea.
- Robot-mower slip and wetness patents (EP4461114, https://data.epo.org/publication-server/rest/v1.2/patents/EP4461114NWA1/document.html; SE542445C2; US12477977). Slip on wet grass and humidity or moisture thresholds are already in industry IP.
- Eder, Prinz, Schöggl & Steinbauer-Wagner, RAS 2023 (https://graz.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/traversability-analysis-for-off-road-environments-using-locomotio). Per-terrain traversability costs from standardized locomotion experiments plus earth-observation data.
- Margolis & Agrawal 2023, Active Sensing Motor Policies (https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01405). Self-supervised friction labels mapped from images on grass, pavement and other terrains. An image-to-friction baseline that the "image-only is blind" claim should engage.
- "Designing Hybrid Mobility for Agricultural Robots", *Machines* 13(7):572 (https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1702/13/7/572). Confirmed by snippet: slopes 0/8/18/28°, soil moisture 10/20/35%, wheeled slip worsens on wet steep ground.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal
- **Wrong authors.** Prior work #6 is attributed to "Fritz et al. (TU Graz)". The RAS 2023 paper (S0921889023001331) is by Eder, Prinz, Schöggl & Steinbauer-Wagner.
