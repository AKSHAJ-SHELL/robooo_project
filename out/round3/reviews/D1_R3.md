# D1 — Reviewer 3 (Impact, venue fit and lab appeal)
Scores: N 6 / F 6.5 / I 6 → overall 6.17

## Novelty (6): The specific 2×2 (leaf film vs soil water) on a 10–15 kg driven robot, run across many lawns with held-out-lawn prediction, does look new. But the proposal says "nobody appears to have run this ... for any vehicle," and that is too strong. The University of Tennessee Biosystems Engineering group (F. Tompkins et al.) tested lawn-and-garden tractor tires on dry and wet turf. Their work includes a 1990 MS thesis (level-ground draft tests on dry and wet turf, plus up-slope "spinout" angle tests), SAE 911240 (1991), which reported that average drive-wheel slip *fell* when water was sprayed on the grass, and a 1996 thesis that used free-surface moisture (true/false) as a factor on tall fescue under constant drawbar load. I saw these as search-result snippets only, because the pages were blocked. That is the canonical turf-vehicle leaf-film experiment, and it already has a slope-limit test. It was missed. Soil-water effects are well established for tractors, cleats and orchard robots (the proposal's refs 4, 5 and 7). What remains new is splitting the variance into within-lawn temporal and between-lawn parts, the tread × wetness interaction at very low wheel loads, and the free-weather-data predictor scored on held-out lawns. That is "known physics in a new regime, with a useful decomposition": 6, not 7.

## Feasibility (6.5): The rig is simple and the irrigation and mist manipulations make it independent of weather. Those are real strengths. The plan is bigger than it looks, though. It needs 14 lawns with written permission (including park lawns), 56 lawn-visits, about 1,340 pulls, pre-school dew sessions, gravimetric calibration for each lawn, slope climbs on 4 lawns, and four model families. All of this falls in roughly 10 weeks of part-time work across the holidays. Tethered drawbar pulls that spin wheels to peak slip will scuff turf, which may lose neighbour and park permissions after the first visit. The main risk (hoverboard current limit before peak traction) is real. The fallback (a towed braked-wheel tester) is effectively a second rig. M1 (image-only) and M4 (proprioceptive) each add work that isn't needed for the headline. The power analysis rests on assumed SDs, but the plan does re-run it after the pilot, which is good.

## Impact (6): One number would be cited: the share of μ_peak variance that is temporal versus between-lawn for small wheeled robots on turf. "Dew derate by X% on lugged vs smooth tread" is a design rule mower makers could use. Still, Husqvarna, Segway and Mammotion run their own internal slope and wet-grass tests, and a 14-lawn study from one California region is unlikely to change their firmware. The research audience is narrow. Vision-slip and traversability people (WayFAST/WayFASTER, Angelova) would see it as a caveat paper, not a method. Fritz et al. and others already list weather as a known confound. The release would be a small table of drawbar pulls, not a perception dataset others can train on, so reuse is limited. The M1 baseline is image-conditioned, but it is trained on only about 1,000 pulls from 10 lawns, so it would be weak by construction. Beating it says little about Angelova or SlipNet in their own settings. For venue fit, I'd call it a solid CASE submission (robotic-mower automation framing) or a strong ICRA-workshop paper. IROS main track would need a striking result, such as temporal share > 50% plus a sign reversal by tread.

## Lab appeal: Weak to moderate, and the two named fits are oversold. Nobby Kobayashi's actual research (UCSC NECTAR lab) is semiconductors, energy conversion and seawater hydrogen. The traversability SIP project is real per out/labs.md, but no traversability publications back it, and SIP runs in summer 2027, after the Mar 1 submission. Carpin's RAPID work (with Goldberg, Viers and Vougioukas, about 2016–2020) is about drip-emitter adjustment and soil-moisture inference in vineyards. Robots there drive on bare or row-crop soil, and drip irrigation does not create the leaf film this study is about. His ICRA 2024 Nav2 paper (Sani, Sgorbissa, Carpin, arXiv 2407.18535) treats tall grass as a lidar false-positive obstacle, not a traction problem. Carpin could give useful feedback on evaluation design, but the "RAPID moisture maps become slip maps" pitch is a stretch he would probably notice.

## Main criticism: The headline novelty claim ("nobody has run this for any vehicle") is wrong in its leaf-film half: UTK/Tompkins already sprayed turf and measured slip and slope spinout on lawn tractors. With that work counted, the contribution narrows to a regime transfer (lighter wheels) plus a variance decomposition, a modest-impact result with a narrow audience and no lab that clearly needs it.

## What would raise the score:
1. Cite and engage the UTK lawn-tractor work (1990 and 1996 theses, SAE 911240). Frame the paper as testing whether their counterintuitive "wet turf reduces slip" result holds, or reverses, at 10–15 kg wheel loads and with leaf versus soil water separated. That turns a missed citation into a sharp, falsifiable hypothesis.
2. Cut scope to what the headline needs. Run about 8 lawns plus 3 held-out ones, keep leaf × soil × tread, and drop M1 or M4 (keep M2 versus M3). Build the braked-wheel tester first as the primary instrument, so you aren't limited by the drive.
3. Get a real external hook. Ask a robot-mower maker's public test lab or a consumer-testing body (Consumer NZ already runs a "uniformly damp" mower test protocol) to use your protocol, or propose a standard "dew derate" test procedure. That gives the result a user beyond the paper.
4. Re-target lab outreach. Kitts (SCU field rovers) or Elkaim (UCSC ASL, ground robots, SIP) are more plausible advisers than Kobayashi. Pitch Carpin only on evaluation design, not RAPID.
5. Make the dataset reusable for perception. Log synchronized images, IMU and motor current for every pull, so traversability researchers can train or test on it, not just read a table.

## Closest prior work found:
- Tompkins et al., "Tractive Performance of Ride-On Lawn and Garden Tractor Tires During Vehicle Operation on Turf," SAE 911240 (1991), https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/911240/: water sprayed on grass reduced drive-wheel slip. Direct overlap with the leaf-film factor (snippet only).
- UTK MS thesis (1990, major prof. F. D. Tompkins), https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/7107: L&G tractor tires, dry versus wet turf, draft load, up-slope spinout angle. Overlaps the leaf-wetness and slope-validation parts (snippet only).
- UTK MS thesis (1996), https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/6831: tractor on tall fescue towing a constant drawbar load, with free-surface moisture as a factor (snippet only; I am not fully certain which of 6831 or 14117 is the 1996 thesis).
- WayFAST, Gasparino et al. 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12071: self-supervised image-to-traction-coefficient prediction, including snow-covered grass. A stronger "image-only" baseline lineage than the one proposed.
- Penn State SSRC turf traction (McNitt), already cited: soil-water–traction sign varies by species. Confirmed by search snippet.
- Cenek, Jamieson & McLarin, https://trid.trb.org/View/783691: a lightly loaded tyre is more wetness-sensitive. Claim confirmed by TRID snippet.
- Sani, Sgorbissa, Carpin, ICRA 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18535: grass as a lidar false obstacle in Nav2. Only loosely related.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal:
- "Nobody appears to have run this 2×2 for any vehicle": the leaf-wetness manipulation on turf has been run for lawn tractors (UTK/Tompkins, 1990–1996). The full 2×2 with soil water separated may still be new.
- Kobayashi framed as a traversability lab: his lab (NECTAR) works on semiconductors and energy. The SIP project title may be accurate, but there is no research line behind it for "the lab gains" to plug into, and SIP timing comes after the deadline.
- RAPID framed as "robots drive irrigated vineyards and the lab predicts soil moisture with ML": broadly right, but RAPID is a Goldberg-led (Berkeley) multi-campus project from about 2016–2020 on drip emitters, not a current Carpin research line, and drip irrigation doesn't produce turf leaf film.
- The Carpin ICRA 2024 paper is described accurately (verified). The Cenek and PSU claims are consistent with search snippets. I could not open sip.ucsc.edu or arXiv to check further.
