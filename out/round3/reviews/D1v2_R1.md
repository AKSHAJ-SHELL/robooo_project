# D1.v2 — Reviewer 1 (Academic depth and novelty)
Scores: N 6 / F 6.5 / I 6 → overall 6.17

## Novelty (6): 
v2 frames the work correctly this time. The UTK lawn-tractor result (SAE 911240; UTK MS thesis 6831, 1996: a Ford lawn tractor on tall fescue towing a constant drawbar load of 0–375 lb, with "free-surface moisture" as a true/false factor, per a TRACE search snippet) is now the null hypothesis rather than something the proposal overlooked. I found no published driven-wheel test at 50–75 N on living turf that separates leaf water from soil water. That specific cell looks open. However, the main mechanism the proposal offers (a water film on short blades acting as the shear plane when contact pressure is low) is old knowledge in aviation. NASA wet-runway work, Pytka et al. on grass airfields, and UK CAA Safety Sense 7B ("if grass is wet and very short, the surface may be slippery and distances may increase by up to 60%") all describe it. Cenek et al. already showed that a lightly loaded dragged tyre is more wetness-sensitive than locked-wheel braking. So "the sign might reverse at low load" is a reasonable, partly anticipated hypothesis, not a surprising new question. Sports-turf work also already documents seasonal and within-site traction variation on natural grass (e.g. Aspetar's study across months and species, and PSU's soil-water studies), which weakens the novelty of Part B's "within-lawn share" in principle, though not for robot wheels. Net: a known measurement method (single-wheel tester, μ–slip curves) applied in a new regime with one clean factorial. That is a 5–6 on the rubric, lifted slightly by the leaf × soil design. I accept the proposal's own caveat that a reviewer may read it as "terramechanics in a new regime".

## Feasibility (6.5): 
Moving to a towed carriage with a fifth wheel and a hub motor in FOC torque mode solves v1's instrument problems. The torque needed (about 60 N × 0.08 m × μ 0.8 ≈ 4 N·m) is well within a hoverboard motor's range. The Part A power arithmetic checks out (SD 0.05·√(2/3) ≈ 0.041; SE ≈ 0.010 with 24 contrasts; power ≈ 0.84 for |Δμ| = 0.03). But inference rests on only 3 lawns, and the sessions are treated as near-independent replicates. Three unverified risks remain. (1) The test wheel's normal load is set by ballast but never measured during a ramp. Load transfer from the hitch line height and caster geometry will change N as traction rises, which biases μ = F/N unless the geometry is controlled or a second load cell is added. (2) December–February is California's rainy season, so the "as-found versus raised" soil contrast and the dry-leaf afternoon state may be hard to schedule. (3) The total load is large for two part-time students: about 12 factorial sessions, 3 dew mornings, about 25 panel sessions, 72 slope stalls, gravimetric calibration on 8 lawns, and permissions. The plan is doable with the stated fallbacks, but it has little slack.

## Impact (6): 
A clean sign result ("leaf water helps, or hurts, 60 N wheels; tread decides") would be a citable design rule for robot-mower and small-UGV designers. The open carriage and protocol add modest reuse value. However, the secondary result, the within-lawn variance share, is pre-registered with a 95% CI width of about 0.5 on 8 lawns. Even in the best case it says only "share > 0.2", which traversability researchers will find suggestive at most. The dataset (about 600 ramps, 8 lawns) is too small for perception groups to adopt. The solid 6.5" hoverboard tyre is also not representative of commercial mower wheels. The likely outcome is a CASE main-track paper if the sign is clean, or an ICRA workshop paper otherwise.

Kitts (SCU RSL) is a plausible fit: an education-focused field-rover lab that could reuse a cheap single-wheel tester. I could not verify the cited Sharma & Kitts IMECE 2023 paper by search. Elkaim's ASL fit is honestly described as a "bridge" and is weak, because no current ASL paper would use this. Asking Carpin for evaluation feedback only is realistic.

## Main criticism: 
The core contribution is a single regime-transfer test (does the tractor-era wet-turf sign hold at about 1/20 of the load?). Its leading mechanism is already well documented for aircraft and lightly loaded tyres on short wet grass, and it is run on 3 lawns with an unrepresentative solid tyre. The more ambitious part, the natural-state variance share and the weather model, is too underpowered at 8 lawns to deliver a strong claim.

## What would raise the score: 
1. Cite and engage the wet-grass aviation literature (NASA, Pytka et al., CAA SS7B) and Cenek's dragged-tyre result. Make the hypothesis quantitative: predict the load or contact pressure at which the sign flips, and test 2–3 normal loads (e.g. 30/60/120 N) so the paper shows the crossover rather than one point.
2. Measure the test wheel's normal load during each ramp (a second load cell, or a parallelogram linkage that removes load transfer), and report μ = F/N(t).
3. Use at least one real robot-mower wheel (a lugged OEM spare part) alongside the hoverboard tyre, so the tread rule transfers to products.
4. Do the Part A factorial on at least 4–5 lawns, even if that means fewer sessions per lawn, so the sign generalizes beyond 3 lawns. Keep the panel at 6 lawns as a secondary, explicitly exploratory result.
5. Check the dew mass (blot paper) against the published dew-amount data for turf (about 0.2 mm, Kentucky creeping-bentgrass study) to justify the mist dose.

## Wrong links/claims in the proposal:
- SlipNet (arXiv 2409.02273) is a planetary-rover, simulation-trained (Vortex Studio) slip predictor for deformable terrain. Listing it next to grass traction models overstates how relevant it is. The link itself is correct.
- Cenek et al.: the "lightly loaded tyre" was a dragged (sliding) part-tyre test, compared against locked-wheel braking. The finding is about test method as much as load, and it does not involve driven rolling wheels. The proposal presents it as clean load-sensitivity evidence.
