---

**Research question.**
- **Primary.** Ride-on lawn-tractor tests at the University of Tennessee found that surface water on turf *reduced* drive-wheel slip. Does that sign hold, vanish or reverse for the 50–75 N wheel loads of 10–15 kg robots, once leaf-surface water (dew or mist) is separated from soil water (rain or irrigation)? Does the answer depend on tread (smooth versus lugged)?
- **Secondary.** On lawns in their natural state, how much of the variation in a small wheel's peak traction is *within-lawn* (time-varying wetness) rather than *between-lawn*? How much of the within-lawn part can free inputs (weather station, time since sunrise, sprinkler log) predict on a lawn the model has never seen?

**Who benefits and how much.**
- **Robot-mower makers and their testers.** Slope ratings are manufacturer claims. Maker guidance on wet grass is qualitative: Navimow tells users to wait for the lawn to dry ([Navimow](https://navimow.com/blogs/navimow-academy/what-to-do-if-your-navimow-slips-on-slopes)). Robot-mower patents already gate on humidity or rain (e.g. EP4461114, per a reviewer's search snippet). None of this says *which* water matters. Consumer NZ's published method tests mowers when grass is "uniformly damp, like with a heavy dew or after a light rain shower", and tests slopes at 19° (35%) ([Consumer NZ, how we test](https://consumer.org.nz/products/robot-lawn-mowers/how-we-test); search snippet). If leaf water and soil water act differently on small wheels, that protocol mixes two different conditions. A measured sign and size is a concrete fix for that kind of protocol and for firmware rules like "derate the slope until dew dries" versus "derate after irrigation".
- **Traversability researchers.** Image-to-traction models (Angelova et al. 2006; WayFAST/WayFASTER; Margolis & Agrawal 2023) treat traction as a function of what the terrain looks like. If the same lawn's traction varies across a day by a share comparable to between-lawn differences, a time or weather input is missing. The released synchronized images, IMU and current with physical μ labels let them test this on their own models.
- **Small-UGV designers** working on grass (park, campus, vineyard inter-row, solar-farm mowing) get a tread × wetness design rule and a cheap tester they can build.

**Why the outcome is uncertain.**
- **The tractor result points one way.** UTK found that water sprayed on turf reduced average drive-wheel slip, and that wet turf gave less rear-wheel slip than dry. Their tests included a constant towed drawbar load and up-slope "spinout" angle tests (SAE 911240, 1991; UTK MS theses [7107](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/7107) and [6831](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/6831); all from search snippets, as the full texts could not be opened).
- **Light-load and sole-friction evidence points the other way.** Cenek et al. found a lightly loaded tyre more wetness-sensitive than a heavily loaded one on grass ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/783691); confirmed by two reviewers' snippets). Work on wet artificial grass found that a water film lowers flat-sole friction and that protrusions restore it ([JEST](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_79983.html)). A 60 N robot wheel at low contact pressure may ride on the leaf canopy, where a water film is the shear plane. A 1+ kN tractor tyre presses through the canopy to the root mat.
- **The soil-water sign is not settled for turf.** Penn State's turf work reports both positive and negative correlations between soil water and traction, depending on species and study ([PSU SSRC report](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf/@@download/file/effects-of-turfgrass-cutting-height-and-soil-conditions-on-traction.pdf)).
- **Theory cannot settle it.** Turf traction mixes rubber–blade friction on a water film, root-mat shear and lug penetration. No small-wheel terramechanics model is validated on living grass. The full set of outcomes is publishable:
  - **Sign holds:** leaf water lowers slip even at 60 N. Dew is not the enemy, and the "wait for dew to dry" advice is wrong for traction.
  - **Sign reverses:** leaf water lowers μ for small wheels, so the tractor-era finding does not transfer. A dew derate is justified.
  - **Tread-dependent:** smooth tyres become film-limited and lugged tyres soil-limited. This is a direct wheel-design rule.
  - **Natural share near zero:** wetness matters under manipulation but not in real lawn states. That would mean terrain-class models are adequate for this regime, also a useful negative.

**Non-obvious insight.** The water that matters for a small robot wheel on grass may be a *hidden temporal state* that a single image does not show. Two simple manipulations separate it from soil water:
- Mist the strip just before testing, which wets the leaves without changing soil water.
- Irrigate 12–20 h before an afternoon test, which wets the soil while the leaves are dry.

The new part is to run both through a true-slip single-wheel tester at small-robot loads, and then check against unmanipulated lawns whether the effect is large enough to matter in practice.

**Closest prior work and what is new.** Unless a link is marked "verified", the claims come from search-result snippets.
1. **UTK lawn and garden tractor turf traction.** SAE 911240, "Tractive Performance of Ride-On Lawn and Garden Tractor Tires During Vehicle Operation on Turf" (1991) ([SAE](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/911240/)), and the UTK MS theses listed above (1990 thesis, major professor F. D. Tompkins per R3's snippet; 1996 thesis on tall fescue with a constant drawbar load and free-surface moisture as a factor). Water sprayed on turf reduced slip. *New:* wheel loads about 20× lower, leaf versus soil water separated, true-slip μ–slip curves, tread as a factor, and a check in natural lawn states.
2. **PSU SSRC (McNitt group), PennFoot.** Cutting height and soil water versus cleated-shoe traction, with a sign that varies by species (report above; [method paper](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/development-and-evaluation-of-a-method-to-measure-traction-on-tur/)). The PSU Supercleat report creates a "wet" condition by irrigating 10 min before testing ([pdf](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/supercleat-report.pdf)). *New:* rolling wheels, not cleats, with leaf and soil water separated.
3. **Cenek, Jamieson & McLarin, frictional characteristics of roadside grass types** ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/783691)). Car tyres, dry versus wet, a species effect, and a lightly loaded tyre being more wetness-sensitive. *New:* driven small wheels, a leaf/soil split, and multiple lawns.
4. **Angelova, Matthies et al., RSS 2006** ([RSS](https://roboticsproceedings.org/rss02/p14.html)). Slip as a function of slope for each visual terrain class. **WayFAST** (Gasparino et al., [arXiv 2203.12071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12071)) and **WayFASTER** ([arXiv 2402.00683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00683), ICRA 2024) predict a self-supervised traction coefficient from images. **SlipNet** ([arXiv 2409.02273](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02273)) does slip prediction from segmentation, and **Margolis & Agrawal 2023** ([arXiv 2311.01405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01405)) estimate friction from images including grass. *New:* we do not retrain these, because our data is too small for that to be fair. We release an image dataset with time-varying physical labels on which they can be tested.
5. **Eder, Prinz, Schöggl & Steinbauer-Wagner, RAS 168 (2023)**, traversability from standardized locomotion experiments plus earth-observation data ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0921889023001331)). *(v1 wrongly attributed this to "Fritz et al.")* The snippets show no explicit wetness factor. *New:* within-site temporal variation.
6. **"Designing Hybrid Mobility for Agricultural Robots", *Machines* 13(7):572 (2025)** ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1702/13/7/572)). Wheeled versus tracked slip at 0/8/18/28° and 10/20/35% soil moisture; wheeled slip worsens on wet, steep ground. This is bare soil, with no leaf film.
7. **US patents "Predicting terrain traversability for a vehicle"** (US11193781, US11156469, US11698264, per R1's snippets). These claim using weather and precipitation data plus slip labels to predict slip. *New:* an open, measured test of whether such inputs work for small wheels on turf, and of which water they need to capture.
8. Single-wheel testers on turf exist in agricultural engineering (e.g. a Teka 13(1) study, snippet only, details unchecked), as do tractor-mounted field testers ([J. Terramech. 1996](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489896000146)). *New:* a hub-motor carriage sized for robot wheels, cheap enough to take to 8 lawns many times.

**Instrument (primary): towed single-wheel carriage.**
- **Carriage.** A small frame with two free casters and one **test wheel**. The test wheel is a hoverboard hub motor (6.5" solid tyre, or the same tyre with a bolt-on TPU lug band), loaded with ballast to **60 N**. That is roughly the per-wheel load of a 12 kg two-wheel-drive robot; a ±15 N check runs in the pilot.
- **Tow and force.** The carriage is towed at about 0.3 m/s by the bin-robot hoverboard base in speed mode, or hand-towed as a backup. The tow link is a 200 kg S-beam load cell, which reads tension and compression.
- **True slip.** An unbraked **fifth wheel** with a quadrature encoder gives ground speed. Wheel speed comes from the hub Hall sensors. For driving, s = 1 − v_ground/(ω r); braking slip is defined the same way.
- **Ramp.** The ESP32 runs the hub motor in torque mode (EFeru FOC firmware). It ramps drive torque from 0 until the wheel spins (s > 0.5) over about 4 s and 1.5 m of travel. That gives one **μ–slip curve**: net traction F/N against s, with carriage rolling resistance measured in a zero-torque pass and subtracted.
- **Outputs.**
  - **μ_peak.**
  - **Slip at F/N = 0.3**, which is comparable to UTK's constant-drawbar-load slip metric.
  - **Initial slope dμ/ds.**
- **Turf damage.** Each ramp scuffs about 1.5 m × 5 cm. Every ramp uses a fresh track 30 cm away, on lawns whose owners have agreed to this in writing.
- **Pilot checks (late Nov).**
  - Load-cell calibration against hanging weights (target ≤ 2% error).
  - Fifth-wheel calibration over a taped 10 m line.
  - Ramp-to-ramp SD of μ_peak on one strip.
  - Sensitivity to tow speed (0.2 versus 0.5 m/s).

**Part A: controlled factorial (mechanism and sign).**
- **Lawns.** 3 (two home lawns and one school field area), chosen to differ in species (for example cool-season fescue or rye versus kikuyu or Bermuda).
- **Factors.**
  - **Leaf water.** Dry, or misted to a target blot mass. The dose is about 0.2–0.3 mm from a garden pump sprayer, checked with a DIY interdigitated leaf-wetness grid and filter-paper blot mass.
  - **Soil water.** As-found, or raised. Raised means about 10 mm of irrigation 12–20 h earlier, with the afternoon test run once the leaves read dry. Soil water is measured with capacitive probes calibrated gravimetrically per lawn (oven-dried cores). If rain makes "as-found" already wet, that week is skipped for the soil contrast. The soil state is always recorded as measured volumetric water content (VWC), not as a label.
  - **Tread.** Smooth or lugged.
- **Replication.** Each lawn gets 2 sessions per soil state, for 12 sessions in total. Each session runs dry-leaf ramps, then mist and a 5 min wait, then wet-leaf ramps: 2 treads × 2 leaf states × 3 ramps = 12 ramps in about 40 min. That makes **144 ramps**, with tread order randomized.
- **Primary test (pre-registered).** The leaf-water contrast Δμ_peak (wet − dry) and Δslip@0.3, paired within session, estimated overall and by tread:
  - **"Sign holds (UTK)":** Δslip@0.3 < 0 with the 95% CI excluding 0.
  - **"Reverses":** Δslip@0.3 > 0 with the CI excluding 0.
  - **"No practical effect":** CI inside ±0.03 in μ_peak.
- The soil contrast and the leaf × soil and leaf × tread interactions come from the same mixed model: μ ~ leaf × soil × tread + (1 | lawn/session).
- **Mist versus dew validation.** On 3 lawns, one weekend morning each:
  - natural dew at dawn: 2 treads × 3 ramps;
  - the same strip once the grid reads dry, about 10–11 am: 2 × 3 ramps;
  - mist to the dawn blot mass: 2 × 3 ramps.

  Compare Δ_dew with Δ_mist. With 6 lawn-tread contrasts this only detects a gross mismatch (|Δ_dew − Δ_mist| ≳ 0.07). Leaf temperature and turgor also differ between dawn and late morning, and we say so. The second, independent check is that the factorial's leaf effect must agree with the dew-versus-dry differences observed in Part B.

**Part B: natural-state observational panel (variance share and weather model).**
- **Lawns.** 8 in total (the 3 from Part A plus 5 more: relatives' and neighbours' lawns with damage consent, and distinct school turf areas). At least 3 species classes are included. Several of the lawns are chosen within walking distance of each other, so one session covers 3–4 lawns.
- **Occasions.** **10 per lawn (80 lawn-occasions)**, at pre-scheduled times assigned before seeing the weather:
  - weekend dawn, about 7:15–8:00 (December sunrise is about 7:15 in Northern California);
  - weekend late morning;
  - weekday after school, 3:30–4:30, before sunset.

  Nothing is manipulated, and the irrigation each owner already runs is logged.
- **Per occasion.** 3 spots × 2 treads = 6 ramps (about 15 min), for **480 ramps** in total. Each occasion also records leaf grid and blot, soil VWC at 3 spots, grass height, air temperature and dew point, plus a downward photo.
- **Load.** About 25 sessions of roughly 1 h each between Dec 7 and Feb 7, which is about 2.5 sessions a week across the two students. Winter break absorbs the dawn weekdays.
- **Variance decomposition.** μ_peak ~ tread + (1 | lawn) + (1 | lawn:occasion) + spot residual. The **natural within-lawn share** is σ²_occasion / (σ²_occasion + σ²_lawn). Its CI comes from a lawn-cluster bootstrap.
- **Prediction (LOLO over 8 lawns, lawn-cluster bootstrap on the RMSE difference).**
  - **M0, static ("terrain-class" stand-in).** Tread plus lawn-level covariates from a photo: species class and cut height.
  - **M2, free inputs.** M0 plus CIMIS dew-point depression, hours since rain, hours since logged irrigation, time since sunrise, and reference ET.
  - **M3, in-situ probe.** M0 plus the leaf-wetness grid and soil VWC.
  - Headline quantities: the RMSE gain of M3 over M0 (how much the hidden state matters) and the fraction of that gain M2 recovers (whether free data is enough). Models are kept to linear or mixed regressions with fewer than 8 terms, because there are only 8 lawns.

**Power (on the headline quantities).** Assumptions are pilot-plausible and re-estimated in the November pilot: ramp SD of μ_peak = 0.05; between-lawn SD = 0.06; natural occasion SD = 0.03–0.06. Part B figures come from our own 200-run Monte Carlo simulation using ANOVA variance components and a lawn-cluster bootstrap.
- **Part A, leaf sign.** There are 24 paired session × tread contrasts, each a difference of 3-ramp means: SD ≈ 0.05·√(2/3) ≈ 0.041, inflated to about 0.05 for session-level noise. SE ≈ 0.010, which gives **power ≈ 0.85 for |Δμ| = 0.03** overall. By tread (n = 12) the SE is 0.014, giving **power ≈ 0.9 for |Δμ| = 0.05**. Inference is within these 3 lawns; generality comes from the panel.
- **Part B, variance share.** With 8 lawns × 10 occasions, the median 95% CI width of the share is **about 0.5**. That is wide, and we do not claim a precise share. The pre-registered claim is "the lower CI bound is above 0.2". This has **power ≈ 0.9 if the true share is 0.5**, and the test is correctly not significant (about 0.15–0.2 false-positive rate at the boundary) if the true share is 0.2.
- **Part B, LOLO RMSE gain of M2 over M0.** Power for the gain's CI to exclude 0 is **about 0.8 when weather explains about 60% of occasion variance and the occasion SD is 0.06**. It falls to **about 0.35–0.5** if weather explains 30% or the occasion SD is 0.03. So the M2 result is reported as an estimate with a CI, and it is a confirmatory claim only in the favourable case. Going from 8 to 10 lawns helps little (0.86 versus 0.78), so the plan stays at 8.

**Slope check (secondary, merged from F2).**
- **Corrected statics.** With driven-axle normal load N_d(θ) computed from the robot's measured CG and wheelbase (casters carry the rest), the largest tow force the robot can sustain on a grade θ is
  F_tow,max = μ_peak·N_d(θ) − W sin θ − c_rr·W cos θ.
  The bare climbing limit is the F_tow,max = 0 case. For a fully driven robot this reduces to μ cos θ ≥ sin θ + c_rr cos θ, i.e. tan θ_max ≈ μ − c_rr. With μ ≈ 0.6 and c_rr ≈ 0.05, that is about 55% grade, above most lawn slopes (R2's point).
- **Getting to the limit anyway.** On 2–3 lawn slopes (flat, about 10%, about 20–25%, measured with an inclinometer on a 1 m board and cross-checked by IMU), the bin-robot base, fitted with the smooth or lugged tread, climbs straight up **towing the braked carriage**. The carriage brake ramps up until the robot stalls, defined as fifth-wheel speed below 10% of commanded. The hitch load cell gives the measured F_tow,max.
- **Comparison.** Predicted (from a flat carriage μ test on the same strip and session) against measured, in dry and dew or mist states: 2 treads × 2 wetness × 3 grades × 3 repeats ≈ 72 stalls. The outputs are a Bland–Altman plot and a test of whether wetness biases the flat-to-slope transfer, which is F2's question.
- Wet smooth tyres may reach the bare limit on the steepest slope without any tow load. If so, that point is reported directly.

**Strongest fair baseline.**
- **Scientific.** The UTK tractor result, i.e. the null hypothesis that small wheels behave like tractor tyres. Also M0, the terrain-class assumption, on the same LOLO folds.
- **Industry.** A constant slope rating with a rain or humidity pause, and the Consumer NZ-style "uniformly damp" condition, which does not separate dew from soil water.
- **Optional commercial check.** If a robot mower can be borrowed from family or neighbours, run its unloaded climb on the same strips at dawn-dew and dry (a few attempts each). This shows whether the carriage result shows up on a real product. We will not claim anything about mower models in general.
- **What we do not do.** We do not retrain WayFAST, SlipNet or Margolis-style models on about 600 ramps. That would be a straw man. The released dataset lets their authors or others test them fairly.

**Main risks and fallbacks.**
- **Carriage ramp is noisy on uneven turf** (SD > 0.06). Fallbacks: average 4 ramps instead of 3, lengthen the ramp to 2 m, and add carriage pitch from the IMU as a covariate.
- **Rain weeks erase the soil contrast.** The soil factor is recorded as measured VWC, irrigation is done on dry weeks, and the panel benefits from rain anyway, because natural variation is what it measures.
- **Dew is absent on scheduled dawns.** That is a real natural state and is kept. Dew is common on clear winter mornings in coastal and inland California valleys, but this is not guaranteed.
- **Turf permission is withdrawn.** Part A and much of Part B run on the team's and the school's own lawns, and a 6-lawn panel is the pre-planned minimum.
