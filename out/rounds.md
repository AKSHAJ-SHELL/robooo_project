## B1.v1: Mechanism instead of perception: capture envelopes of a passive self-centering hitch for unmodified 64/96-gal carts

Median overall **5.83**, status: ELIMINATED. Angle text: [`angles/B1.v1.md`](angles/B1.v1.md).

### Reviewer 1 (Academic depth): N 6 · F 5.5 · I 6 → overall 5.83

- **Novelty 6:** I found no prior capture envelope on unmodified ANSI refuse carts across brands, sizes and fills, so the domain-specific measurement is new. However, the core thesis has been studied before. Measuring the allowable positioning error for a compliant mechanism to argue that it removes the need for sensing is exactly Dollar & Howe's IJRR 2010 SDM-hand evaluation (doi 10.1177/0278364909360852), and passive docking connectors with converging guides, springs and latches routinely report misalignment tolerance (ROBIO 2022, Industrial Robot 2023). The contribution is therefore a known evaluation method applied to a new setting, plus a cost conversion.
- **Feasibility 5.5:** The parts are hobbyist and the core build (hoverboard base, printed V-guides, a latch, an HX711 load cell) is well trodden. The experiment plan is the problem. It calls for about 1,400 grid trials, 90 sensing-error approaches and 100 end-to-end runs, plus three brands, two sizes, three fills and the wall and slope subsets. At 30 s per trial that is only 12 h, but resetting the cart, jig and sandbags realistically takes 1-2 min, which means 25-50 h of outdoor testing in winter on school weekends. The three V-guide angles (30/45/60 deg) are also not in the trial count. On top of this come several risks: flashing the hoverboard firmware, the backordered load cell, and tilting the cart 'by its own motion' with no lift actuator.
- **Impact 6:** A clean logistic-regression capture envelope with bootstrap CIs and an end-to-end validation is a respectable CASE, ARSO or ICRA-workshop paper and a good application story. An IROS main-track acceptance is unlikely, because the result (a funnel beats a rigid hook) is expected, the baseline is weak, and the compliance-vs-sensing literature (Dollar & Howe, RISC robotics) is not engaged.
- **Main criticism:** The headline finding, that a converging self-centering guide tolerates more approach error than a rigid hook, is predictable from compliance and funnel theory and from Dollar & Howe's positioning-error-tolerance experiments. The comparison is also against a straw-man baseline: a hook modeled on an abandoned patent, while a commercial passive handle hook (Garbage Commander) already exists. Reviewers will therefore see the novelty as domain-only. The 'sensing dollars' link is shaky because the cheap lidar's own accuracy (+/-45 mm) may not reach the precision the rigid hook needs.
- **What would raise the score:** 1. Turn it from 'funnel beats hook' into a design-law paper. Derive the capture envelope analytically from guide convergence angle, friction coefficient and cart mass/inertia (cart-shove vs center condition, extending Whitney/Mason-style analysis to a free-rolling cart), then validate that model with a much smaller, well-chosen trial set: 2-3 guide angles x 1-2 brands x 2 fills, about 300-400 trials total.
2. Benchmark against the strongest cheap alternative (the Garbage Commander-style passive hook), not an abandoned patent's rigid hook.
3. Cite and position against Dollar & Howe 2010 and RISC robotics.
4. Measure the actual terminal docking error of each sensing configuration before claiming the dollar trade-off.
Together these would raise novelty and impact and cut the trial load to something feasible.

### Reviewer 4 (Methodology rigor): N 5.5 · F 6 · I 6 → overall 5.83

- **Novelty 5.5:** No one appears to have mapped capture envelopes for unmodified ANSI refuse carts across brands, sizes and fills, and the 'sensing dollars per hitch' link is a fresh evaluation. The core thesis is not new, though. Choi et al. 2006 (doi 10.1115/detc2006-99054) already designed a passive docking mechanism to absorb +/-5 cm and +/-30 deg of homing error without sensors. Hagen et al. 2026 (doi 10.36227/techrxiv.177160636.66952573/v1) built a passive coupling for 'minimal sensing' that tolerates 3-12 cm. Funnel-instead-of-sensing goes back to Erdmann & Mason 1986 and Canny & Goldberg 1994. What is left is a known principle applied to a new object, with careful measurement.
- **Feasibility 6:** The hardware is simple: no perception is needed for the grid trials, and the parts are hobby-grade. The experiment matrix is the problem. The stated grid is 13 lateral x 7 yaw x 2 speeds = 182 cells, so 5-rep screening alone is 910 trials per hitch, not the stated ~700. The 30 s per trial ignores re-placing a 90 kg-filled cart, unlatching, re-jigging yaw and taking the photo. At a realistic ~2 min per trial, 1,400 trials is about 47 h, before adding the brand, size, fill, wall, slope, sensing and end-to-end subsets. Two other risks stack on top: flashing EFeru hoverboard firmware, and borrowing three cart brands. All of this falls in a part-time school-term window.
- **Impact 6:** This is a clean, quantitative, low-cost study and a reasonable fit for the IEEE CASE or an ICRA workshop. It would make a good college-application story: a real mechanism with real statistics. For IROS main track, a reviewer will probably call the headline result geometrically foregone: a funnel's capture width is roughly its mouth width minus the handle width. Without a predictive model of the centre-versus-shove condition, the paper reads as an engineering characterization, not a robotics contribution.
- **Main criticism:** The design has three methodology problems.
1. The rigid-hook-versus-funnel comparison is nearly determined by geometry, so the 3x hypothesis is close to a straw man. The scientifically interesting variables are the V-guide angle (30/45/60 deg, built but missing from the experiment matrix) and the friction and mass condition under which the cart centres instead of being shoved. Neither is modelled or systematically varied.
2. The sensing-cost link has a confound. Terminal docking error is measured from a 'taught start pose', but in practice the dominant error is where the truck or a person left the cart, not robot odometry. Unless cart placement is sampled from a measured, realistic distribution, the 'sensing dollars' result does not answer the question.
3. The statistics are too thin for the claims. In 50 end-to-end trials, even 50/50 successes gives a Wilson 95% lower bound of only 92.9%, and 48/50 gives [86.5%, 98.9%]. So '>=95% engagement' cannot be shown. Brand, size and fill are tested only on subsets, not crossed with the full grid, so the falsification test 'brand explains more variance than hitch type' is confounded with which grid cells each brand saw.
Also, the 'open-loop blind approach' means the commanded offset is not the actual offset at contact. Ground-truth contact pose from the overhead photo must be the regressor, not the jig setting.
- **What would raise the score:** Recast the paper as model plus validation, and cut the experiment matrix.
- Derive a simple quasi-static condition, Whitney/Mason-style: whether the cart centres or slides, as a function of guide angle, friction coefficient, cart mass and fill, and wheel rolling resistance. This predicts the capture half-width.
- Validate it on a reduced grid with adaptive boundary sampling: about 300-400 trials per hitch, with V-angle as a factor.
- Use the measured contact pose (overhead photo or AprilTag) as the regressor.
- Measure the real distribution of post-pickup cart placement over 3-4 collection days and convolve against that.
- Report the end-to-end results with an honest Wilson CI, targeting '>=90%'. Alternatively, run about 150 trials if a 95% claim is required.
- Cite Choi 2006 and Hagen 2026 as the closest 'mechanism instead of sensing' precedents, and position the paper as the first model-validated envelope on deformable, rolling, unmodified refuse carts.
- Target CASE rather than IROS.
- **Wrong links/costs in the proposal:** I found no wrong links. The JASAE abstract matches (80 trials, 20% day / 65% night, 35-L bin, ESP32). The Whitney 1982 title, author and journal match Crossref for doi 10.1115/1.3149634. arXiv 2110.06648 and PMC11408198 resolve, and their figures (0.03 m / 0.02 rad; TF40 + QR, under 5 mm) match prior_art.md. US20200023524A1 resolves. The other Google Patents links returned 503 (rate limiting), so I did not re-verify them myself. Arithmetic error: the grid as specified gives 182 cells x 5 reps = 910 screening trials per hitch, not 'about 700'. The 30 s per trial and 12 h total are optimistic by roughly 3-4x. The total cost of about $620 is plausible. Note that the SparkFun 200 kg load cell is backordered, as the proposal itself says. ### Reviewer 5 (Cost-claim audit): N 6.5 · F 4 · I 5.5 → overall 5.33

- **Novelty 6.5:** Nothing I could check measures end-to-end task success against hardware dollars across nested sensor and compute tiers on one physical robot. The closest work is either car-scale and dataset-based (Collin & Teran Espinoza) or one-tier with no cost axis (Hamzeh & Prasetyo 2026: 80 trials, 20%/65% success). Two things keep the score below 7: 'add-one-sensor' ablations are routine in robotics papers, so the twist is mainly the dollar axis; and my own search was cut short (web-search quota exhausted, arXiv/OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar rate-limited), so absence claims are weakly bounded. Theory papers comparing the power of sensor configurations (e.g. O'Kane & LaValle's robot-dominance work, UNVERIFIED, not opened) are uncited.
- **Feasibility 4:** Two part-time high-school students would need to build in about 5-6 weeks (Dec 1-Jan 10): MCU dead reckoning, visual teach-and-repeat, a custom cart detector, an AprilTag EKF, lidar ranging, RTK fusion, and autonomous hitching of unmodified carts. Then 175 round trips by Feb 20. That is the heaviest plan on the board. On top of that, the headline TOST test is infeasible at n=25. My simulation (Newcombe 90% CI inside ±10 points) gives equivalence power of 0.0% at a true 80% success rate for both tiers, 0.6% at 90%, 7.6% at 95%, and 100% only if both tiers are perfect. Roughly 110-140 trials per arm would be needed.
- **Impact 5.5:** A clean, matched cost-versus-success frontier with a HardwareX-style BOM is a good fit for CASE, ARSO or an ICRA workshop. It is also a strong application story ('we measured what each dollar buys'). For IROS main track, one primary driveway, n=25 per tier, an equivalence test that will almost surely be inconclusive, and a likely fallback to per-subtask results make acceptance unlikely. And if the build slips, the five-tier comparison collapses to three tiers or to sub-task results.
- **Main criticism:** The study is overbuilt and underpowered at the same time. Five full autonomy stacks (including visual teach-and-repeat, RTK fusion and autonomous hitching of unmodified carts) cannot realistically be integrated by two part-time high-school students by Jan 10. Even if they are, the headline claim (C3 equivalent to C5 within ±10 points by TOST at n=25) has under 8% power unless both tiers are near-perfect. The likely outcome is an inconclusive equivalence test on a frontier that has shrunk to three tiers or to sub-task results. Also, the cost-per-success metric (successes per $100 of BOM) is dominated by its denominator, so it will favor C1 whenever success rates are close.
- **What would raise the score:** Cut to three tiers (C1 MCU-only, C3 camera+tags, C5 RTK) with one shared hitching and placement module.

Replace TOST with a pre-registered superiority / non-inferiority design whose sample size is actually computed. One option is a one-sided non-inferiority test with a 15-20 point margin and about 40-60 trials per tier. Another is a sub-task decomposition (exit, curb placement, return) with continuous placement-error metrics, which carry far more statistical power per trial than binary success.
